The Gas Detector Passed. Which Alarms Did the Test Prove?

M. Zurix Mohamed, Shafiq Shahari and M. Syukri Mokhtar

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-10

Summary

A four-gas detector can pass calibration while its service record leaves an alarm-test question open. An anonymised example shows how to read alarm settings, challenge concentrations and as-found readings for each sensor.

Full article

The carbon monoxide channel on a four-gas detector calibration sheet carries three numbers: 30 ppm, 200 ppm and 100 ppm. The first two are alarm settings. The third is the concentration of the gas used for calibration. Beside them is a single word: PASS.

Suppose someone asks whether the 200 ppm alarm operated during the test. The 100 ppm gas was sufficient to cross the 30 ppm threshold, but it was below 200 ppm. The sheet records a pass for the channel. To establish how the high alarm was verified, we would need the test procedure or a separate alarm-test record.

That is a useful question to ask when reviewing a calibration report. Each number tells us something different about what was tested.

Read the report one gas at a time

The figures below come from an anonymised historical calibration sheet. They illustrate how to read this particular record. Alarm settings for an instrument in service should be checked against the approved configuration for its intended duty.

Carbon monoxide (CO) Alarm settings: 30 ppm and 200 ppm. Calibration gas: 100 ppm CO.

Hydrogen sulphide (H₂S) Alarm settings: 5 ppm and 10 ppm. Calibration gas: 25 ppm H₂S.

Methane (CH₄) Alarm settings: 10% LEL and 20% LEL. Calibration gas: 50% LEL methane.

Oxygen (O₂) Alarm settings: 19.5% low and 23.5% high, by volume. Calibration gas: 18% oxygen by volume.

The oxygen row is particularly easy to misread. An 18% oxygen mixture challenges the detector in the low-oxygen direction. It can help verify the low alarm at 19.5%. It cannot, on its own, demonstrate operation of the high alarm at 23.5%. If verification of both alarms is required, the record should identify how each was checked under the manufacturer’s procedure.

The methane row raises a different issue. Here, 50% LEL means half the lower explosive limit for methane. Writing only “50% methane” in a maintenance record changes the meaning of the figure. Carry the unit through from the cylinder label to the worksheet, particularly when the same test mixture specifies oxygen in percent by volume and toxic gases in parts per million.

Record what the instrument actually showed

The sheet gives the reference gas concentrations and a pass result for each channel. A useful addition to the underlying worksheet is the reading as found, before any adjustment, and as left, after adjustment or verification.

Consider an illustrative entry. The cylinder contains 100 ppm CO, and the detector initially displays 94 ppm. Recording both values preserves the six-ppm difference. Whether that result is acceptable depends on the tolerance specified for that instrument. If an adjustment is made, record the final displayed value as well. A technician investigating drift at the next service can then compare actual readings across visits.

The same principle applies to traceability. Match the detector serial number on the sheet to the unit in hand. For the test gas, record the cylinder identifier, certified concentrations, units and expiry date. The example sheet includes a cylinder reference and a test-procedure reference. Those details give the reviewer a way to retrieve the supporting records behind the stamp.

Before the detector goes back to work

A calibration report records the instrument’s condition on the test date. A pre-use check answers a question about the detector today. A bump test checks that gas reaches the sensors and activates the alarms. A calibration check compares the displayed readings with a known gas concentration. For portable monitors, published guidance recommends a bump test or calibration check before each day’s use, following the manufacturer’s instructions. A failed check calls for full calibration before use.

A practical handover question is: “Show me how the high CO alarm was checked.” A good record lets the technician point to the method and the observed result. That is what gives the word PASS its value.

Technical reference: U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Administration, Calibrating and Testing Direct-Reading Portable Gas Monitors. https://www.osha.gov/publications/shib093013
