Which Tank Gauge Should We Check First?

M. Zurix Mohamed and Aizat Farhan Rohizat

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-10

Summary

Across 149 tanks, the instrumentation team compared level readings with actual tank movements to decide which gauges needed a site visit. PI history and Excel helped the team spot early signs of trouble and give technicians a specific event to investigate.

Full article

During the COVID restrictions, our instrumentation team had 149 tanks to look after. We wanted to cut down on trips to the field, but a technician still had to go out when a gauge needed attention. The question was how to choose. If we sent someone to one tank this morning, what evidence could we give them that made it worth the visit?

A flat level reading is a good example. Open the trend for a storage tank that has been idle overnight and a straight line is exactly what you would expect. Now take that same straight line and put it alongside a transfer into the tank. If enough product has arrived to produce a measurable rise and the reading still hasn’t moved, the technician has a specific question to investigate. The trace looks identical in both cases. What changes its meaning is what happened in the tank.

What we could see from the control room

Our tanks used three measurement technologies: displacers, guided-wave radar and radar. We had more than ten years of instrument history in the PI System. We used Excel to examine that history, which meant we could start with tools already available to the team.

We looked for four things: how often an instrument reported a bad process value, whether the signal disappeared abruptly, whether the reading jumped beyond what the instrument’s accuracy could explain, and whether it stayed fixed during confirmed tank movement. An operator could help us interpret that last pattern in a way a spreadsheet alone never could.

The least useful instruction we could give a technician was “check the gauge.” A much better one was “look at the period when this tank was receiving product; the level stayed flat throughout.” That gave the operator and the technician the same event to work from. It also gave us something we could check afterwards: did the field findings support our reading of the trend?

How much warning is enough?

We spent about six months developing the initial rules and another six months testing them. The corrections took a further six months before we retested. One decision that came out of that work was to give the team two levels of warning. An early warning prompted a closer look at the trend and the tank’s operating history. A more urgent indication called for a field check using the team’s checklist.

In some cases, we could see signs of trouble roughly four months ahead of failure. I would be careful about turning that figure into a maintenance deadline. The useful part of those four months was the opportunity to investigate while the gauge was still returning a reading. Once the signal had gone bad and stayed bad, our options were already narrower.

What I took from those 149 tanks was a habit I still use. When a level trend looks suspiciously steady, my first question is for operations: what was the tank actually doing during those hours? The answer can explain the straight line. It can also tell us exactly where the next site visit should begin.
