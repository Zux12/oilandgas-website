The Pipe You Did Not Inspect

Oil & Gas Editorial

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-06

Summary

The Chevron Richmond fire illustrates why inspection coverage matters as much as inspection frequency. Individual components can deteriorate differently within the same piping circuit.

Full article

Based on a published US Chemical Safety Board (CSB) investigation. The discussion below is Oil & Gas Editorial commentary on the investigation, not a firsthand account.

WHAT HAPPENED

On 6 August 2012, a pipe failed at Chevron’s Richmond refinery, releasing flammable hydrocarbons that subsequently ignited. The investigation identified severe wall thinning from sulfidation corrosion.

A central issue was the possibility of different corrosion rates among individual components in a carbon steel piping circuit. Low-silicon components can be especially vulnerable in this service. The CSB’s recommendations addressed identifying susceptible circuits, inspecting individual components and considering replacement with more corrosion-resistant materials. The final investigation report was released in January 2015.

THE RELIABILITY LESSON

An inspection programme can produce a large amount of data while still leaving an important question unanswered. Does the programme adequately cover the components most likely to deteriorate? The number of completed measurements is useful for tracking work, but it cannot establish that every relevant damage mechanism has been addressed.

This is a useful case for discussing the difference between an inspection location and the equipment condition it is assumed to represent. A reading belongs to a particular location at a particular time. Extending that result to neighbouring components requires an engineering basis, especially where material properties or service conditions may differ.

QUESTIONS FOR THE INTEGRITY TEAM

The first editorial question is how the organisation records uncertainty. If a component’s material history is incomplete, does that uncertainty remain visible in the inspection plan? Or does it disappear when the equipment is grouped into a larger circuit?

A second question concerns the connection between inspection and maintenance decisions. When an inspection identifies deterioration, the engineering conclusion should be understandable to the people who must act on it. A technical report becomes more useful when the required action, responsible owner and follow-up arrangements are clear.

A third question is whether repeated measurements are improving understanding. Revisiting familiar monitoring points may be convenient, but a review should also ask whether those points still provide the evidence needed for the identified damage mechanism. Changes to the inspection strategy should be made by the responsible integrity specialists using applicable requirements and service-specific information.

A PRACTICAL DISCUSSION

At the next reliability meeting, select one piping circuit and ask the team to explain why its inspection locations were chosen. The purpose is to examine the reasoning and any unresolved assumptions, rather than merely confirm that the scheduled work was completed.

TAKEAWAY

Inspection effectiveness depends on coverage, interpretation and follow-through. The Richmond case encourages engineers to look beyond the thickness readings already available and ask what those readings may leave unknown.
