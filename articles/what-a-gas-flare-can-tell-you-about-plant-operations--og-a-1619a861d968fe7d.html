What a Gas Flare Can Tell You About Plant Operations

Oil & Gas Editorial

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-27

Summary

July 2026 edition · A visible flame can mark several very different events. Understanding the source, duration and cause of a flare tells operators much more than its appearance alone.

Full article

Two photographs taken from outside a plant may show almost identical flames. One could coincide with a brief process upset. The other could reflect a recurring constraint that prevents gas from being recovered. The photographs record an observation. The operating history explains what the observation means.

WHY GAS REACHES A FLARE

A flare system provides a controlled destination for gas in circumstances defined by the facility’s design. The industry distinguishes routine, non-routine and safety flaring. Planned maintenance, an interruption to gas handling and a safety-related release have different causes and call for different follow-up. A small pilot flame also serves a different purpose from the waste gas being assessed.

A flare’s apparent size depends partly on the observer’s position and conditions at the time. A photograph cannot establish how much gas flowed, its composition, how efficiently it burned or which upstream equipment sent it. Describing the sight as evidence of a particular failure would go beyond what the observer knows.

FROM EVENT TO EXPLANATION

A useful review places flare-flow information beside the plant event log. When did the increase begin? What operating change occurred before it? Was the flow measured or estimated? A short event with a high peak and a modest continuous flow can look quite different on a trend. Over a month the modest flow can account for more gas.

Imagine a compressor returning to service after maintenance. If flare flow fell at the same time, the timing is a useful lead. The team still needs to establish which streams were routed where and whether other changes occurred during the shift. Once the cause is understood, an operator can assess opportunities for recovery or greater equipment availability while preserving the flare’s safety function.

THE CAMERA AND THE SHIFT LOG

One question worth asking after a recurring event is whether the reporting interval obscures it. A monthly total can look unremarkable while the shift log records a short flare event after the same operation on several occasions. Putting those times side by side can reveal a recurring pattern worth investigating. Recording the basis and uncertainty of the flow figure also helps the next reviewer distinguish an operational improvement from a change in how the quantity was estimated.

TAKEAWAY

The most informative flare report pairs quantity and duration with an operating explanation. That gives the public a more meaningful account of an event and gives the plant a concrete basis for reducing avoidable flaring.
