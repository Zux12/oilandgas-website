Following a Barrel of Crude from the Wellhead to the Export Terminal

Oil & Gas Editorial

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-27

Summary

July 2026 edition · A barrel can change hands several times before reaching a tanker. Its journey is easiest to follow through measurements, inventory records and the points where streams meet.

Full article

By the time a tanker receives a cargo, an individual barrel from a particular well may have mixed with production from several fields. Yet a producer can still be credited for its share. To understand how, follow the records that travel alongside the oil.

AT THE FIELD

What leaves a producing well can contain oil, gas and water. Surface equipment separates the streams sufficiently for their next destinations. Where several wells feed a common facility, a test separator or another allocation measurement can help estimate each well’s contribution. That allocation figure answers a field-management question. A later custody measurement answers a commercial question governed by the relevant agreement.

Gathering lines take production towards a collection point. A tank may receive a steady inflow across a day and deliver its contents to a pipeline in a shorter movement. This is the first reason the daily production and dispatch totals can differ even when every instrument is working.

AT THE TERMINAL

The terminal receives a quantity into inventory and eventually transfers a nominated cargo. Different owners may hold shares in a common grade. The accounts must preserve those entitlements even after the liquids have mixed. Quantity, quality and timing all matter. A cargo sold as one grade must also meet that grade’s agreed characteristics.

For a simple reconciliation, imagine 8,000 barrels arriving while 10,000 leave a terminal over the same interval. If those are the only movements and both volumes share a measurement basis, the missing 2,000 barrels should come out of opening inventory. Starting with a search for a leak would skip the most obvious explanation. Conversely, an inventory adjustment still needs support from tank records, transfer times and the quantity basis used.

THE BARREL THAT CROSSES MIDNIGHT

A terminal with a midnight reporting cut-off can be halfway through loading a vessel when its accounting day closes. The first portion belongs in one day’s physical balance and the remainder in the next. The bill of lading may still describe a single cargo. A reconciliation works best when it retains the transfer identifier across both days. That reference lets someone trace a difference back to a movement, even if the operations report and the commercial statement have different totals.

TAKEAWAY

Ask where the measurement was made, which period it covers, and whether it represents production, a change in storage or a transfer of custody. Those three questions make a barrel traceable on paper long after its physical identity has disappeared.
