When Ship and Shore Disagree on How Much Oil Was Delivered

Oil & Gas Editorial

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-27

Summary

August 2026 edition · A tanker and a terminal can both have credible figures for the same cargo. Reconciling them starts with the transfer boundary, the time window and the quantity each side actually reported.

Full article

The terminal reports a larger delivery than the vessel reports receiving. Both figures have been checked for arithmetic errors. Before anyone reaches for a meter calibration certificate, the most useful move is to write a short definition beside each number. Does it describe volume at observed temperature, volume corrected to a reference temperature, or mass? Does it include water? When exactly did the measurement start and end?

MAKE THE TWO ACCOUNTS COMPARABLE

Oil expands as it warms. A volume measured in a warm shore tank can differ from its volume aboard ship even if the same mass arrived. Temperature observations, density information and the agreed conversion basis belong with the quantities. A result rounded for a commercial document may also differ slightly from an unrounded working figure.

Next comes the physical boundary. Shore inventory may be derived from tank movements or a custody meter. The vessel measures its own tanks and accounts for quantities already on board, where applicable. A transfer line between those two boundaries can start and finish with different contents. A difference held in that line appears in the comparison without having disappeared from the system.

LOOK FOR A PATTERN

A single discrepancy and a consistent bias over many voyages deserve different investigations. A vessel experience factor, where appropriate under the governing arrangements, brings historical ship and shore relationships into the discussion. It needs suitable voyage records. Applying an old factor mechanically could conceal a new cause.

Consider a loading that crosses midnight. The vessel closes its cargo record after completion, while a daily terminal report stops at midnight. Both documents may be internally correct, yet neither is a valid comparator for the other until the reporting periods are reconciled. This elementary check often belongs ahead of a more sophisticated uncertainty calculation.

MAKE THE DIFFERENCE VISIBLE

If shore records 100,000 units and the ship records 99,800 on the same declared basis, the initial difference is 200 units, or 0.2 per cent of the shore figure. Write down that denominator. Then keep each supported adjustment on a separate line, including its sign and source. The calculation is an illustration, not a tolerance for acceptance. A transparent reconciliation lets another person reproduce what was compared instead of having to accept a single corrected total.

TAKEAWAY

Keep the original observations, transfer times and line condition with the calculation. An unexplained difference is a reason to investigate. The commercial agreement and supporting evidence determine which quantity governs the transaction.
