Why Two Crude Oils Can Sell for Different Prices on the Same Day

Oil & Gas Editorial

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-27

Summary

July 2026 edition · A benchmark gives buyers a reference. Quality, refinery capability, freight and delivery terms help explain why two physical cargoes can trade at different prices.

Full article

A buyer sees two offers on the same morning. One cargo is light and low in sulphur but a long voyage away. The other is heavier, contains more sulphur and can arrive before the refinery’s current stock runs low. The benchmark on the trading screen is identical for both offers. The refinery’s calculation is quite different.

WHAT THE BARREL CAN BECOME

Crudes contain different proportions of material boiling across different temperature ranges. A refinery uses crude assay data to estimate what a particular feed will yield and how much further processing those streams may require. API gravity describes relative density. Sulphur content gives another useful indicator. Neither number, on its own, supplies the whole assay.

A refinery equipped to convert heavy fractions and remove sulphur may find a discounted heavier crude attractive. A simpler refinery might value the same cargo less because its equipment gives it fewer options for those fractions. Even at one refinery, the calculation can change when a conversion unit is out for maintenance or when the products customers want change.

WHAT IT COSTS TO GET THERE

A price comparison also needs a common delivery point and period. Freight, insurance where applicable, timing and access to pipelines or terminals all affect what the buyer pays to get a usable cargo to the gate. Comparing a price at the loading port with a delivered offer can mistake a transport cost for a quality premium.

An instructive exercise is to leave the headline price off the first line of a comparison sheet. Start with the delivery month, location, grade and available refinery units. Add the expected value of the products and the costs of getting the cargo there and processing it. Only then bring the two offer prices back into the discussion.

THE REFINER’S PENCIL TEST

Put the offers on a delivered basis and imagine that the heavier cargo saves two dollars per barrel. If the expected difference in saleable product value and extra processing cost together amount to three dollars per barrel, the apparent bargain has disappeared. These are illustrative numbers. Their value is in exposing the assumption that matters. If the calculation reverses only when one product price changes, the buyer now knows which market movement to watch before committing to the cargo.

TAKEAWAY

A crude price differential reflects a particular barrel in a particular place and market. To explain it, identify the buyer’s processing options and delivery constraints as well as the properties of the oil.
