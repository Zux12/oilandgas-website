What a Blocked Impulse Line Taught Me About Flow Measurement

M. Zurix Mohamed

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-05

Summary

A differential-pressure flow transmitter measures the pressures that reach it through its impulse lines. In one heavy-oil service, recurring blockages caused actual plant trips. The most revealing evidence came after the instrument had been removed and taken to the workshop.

Full article

A healthy transmitter can still give a misleading reading When a flow signal trips a process unit, one of the first questions in the control room is, “Did the transmitter fail?” In heavy-oil service, I learned to ask a second question just as early. Could the pressure from the process still reach both sides of the transmitter?

A differential-pressure flow transmitter relies on two small-bore lines to carry pressure from the process taps to its high- and low-pressure ports. If heavy material cools and restricts one of those lines, the pressure at that port can lag behind a change in the process. The instrument may respond correctly to the pressure it receives while giving the operator a misleading picture of the flow.

That distinction mattered in a refinery where I worked. Some of our heavy-oil flow measurements fed protective trips configured as one-out-of-one. A single measurement channel supplied the trip signal. We experienced actual trips associated with blockages, and it could take three to four days for the unit to return to steady operation. What we found in the workshop After a trip, the maintenance team would often install a replacement transmitter and take the removed unit to the workshop. The replacement helped restore service. Understanding why the trip had happened required a closer look at what came off the line.

During dismantling, technicians found blockages in the impulse tubing and in the transmitter manifold. Clearing the deposits and applying heat restored the reading. A calibration check on a clean transmitter could easily miss that evidence. By then, the condition we needed to understand had already been removed.

That experience changed how I read a maintenance record. An entry saying “transmitter replaced, reading restored” tells me what got the measurement back. An as-found note identifying which line was blocked and where the deposit sat tells me what to investigate before the next trip. Why a satisfactory temperature reading left a question open The affected installations had steam tracing and insulation. The insulation was intact, and temperatures checked at the traced tubing during normal weather met the expected range. We had no equivalent measurement during rain. That left a gap in what we knew about the installation over a full range of operating conditions. It would have been easy to blame the weather, but the evidence only established the blockage.

The team prioritised the installations with a history of problems and replaced their steam tracing with electrical heat tracing. Through 2023, we saw no recurrence of clogging at the modified points. That observation applies to those installations. It gave us a practical basis for prioritising similar trouble spots elsewhere.

The lesson I carried forward was about the boundary of a measurement. On an instrument drawing, it is easy to focus on the transmitter symbol. In the field, the measurement begins at the process taps and includes every length of tubing, the manifold, the tracing and the insulation. When the next flow signal behaves unexpectedly, my first request is simple: before the lines are cleared, record what was found and exactly where it was found.
