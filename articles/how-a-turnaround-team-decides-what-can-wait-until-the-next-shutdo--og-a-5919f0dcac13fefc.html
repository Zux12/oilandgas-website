How a Turnaround Team Decides What Can Wait Until the Next Shutdown

Oil & Gas Editorial

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-27

Summary

October 2026 edition · A crowded shutdown scope forces choices. A defensible deferral connects equipment condition, the time to the next opportunity and the person accountable if the underlying assumption changes.

Full article

A vessel is coming out of service for the first time in several years. Inspection wants access, operations wants a modification, and maintenance has a growing list of repairs. The scheduler can show that all the jobs will not fit. Deciding which work can wait requires evidence that sits outside the schedule.

WHAT IS THIS OPPORTUNITY WORTH?

Start with the reason each job is in the shutdown. Some work fulfils statutory or inspection obligations. Some addresses known degradation or restores a safety function. Other jobs improve efficiency or can be carried out safely during normal operation. A common label such as “mechanical work” conceals very different consequences of delay.

Consider two apparently similar exchanger jobs. One bundle has a documented history of fouling, a credible operating trend and a standby arrangement available for the coming run. The other has unexplained deterioration and little evidence about its remaining condition. The same number of hours removed from the schedule represents quite different decisions. Even the standby arrangement deserves scrutiny if it shares the same service or maintenance history.

WRITE DOWN THE ASSUMPTION

A practical deferral record states what is known, which uncertainty remains, the permitted period and the basis for accepting it. It identifies the competent decision maker and any required inspection or regulatory agreement. A date entered in a maintenance system does not by itself establish that the item is fit for the next operating period.

The review should also identify what would reopen the decision. A change in condition, a delayed next turnaround or loss of the standby equipment may invalidate the original reasoning. Assigning an owner gives that change somewhere to go before the next planning meeting.

COUNT THE WAIT AS WELL AS THE WORK

An inspection might take a few hours once a vessel is open, yet gaining access can require a much longer outage. Record that distinction when comparing proposals. A job that looks small on a labour estimate may be impractical between planned shutdowns. Equally, a long preparatory task may be possible while the plant runs. A credible scope decision therefore describes the next realistic access opportunity, rather than assuming the next blank space on a calendar will do.

TAKEAWAY

Ask the team to explain one deferred item as if the next shutdown had just slipped by six months. If the answer changes, the original decision depended on timing. That dependency should be visible alongside the work order, its review date and the evidence supporting continued service.
