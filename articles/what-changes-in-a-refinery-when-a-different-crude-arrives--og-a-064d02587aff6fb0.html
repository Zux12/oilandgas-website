What Changes in a Refinery When a Different Crude Arrives?

Oil & Gas Editorial

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-27

Summary

September 2026 edition · A switch in crude supply can change product yields, hydrogen demand and the load on downstream units. A cheaper cargo is useful only if the whole refinery can process it profitably.

Full article

A refinery can run the same number of barrels through its front gate and send a different mix of products out the back. The explanation often begins with the crude slate. Each crude contains its own distribution of light, middle and heavy material. Changing the blend changes the work that falls on the units beyond the crude distillation tower.

READ THE ASSAY AS A SET OF QUESTIONS

The assay describes properties and expected yields over boiling ranges. A planner can use it to estimate how much naphtha, kerosene, gas oil and heavier material the refinery might produce. The estimates need the actual unit configuration and intended operating plan to become a useful prediction. The same crude can have a different commercial value at another refinery.

Sulphur illustrates the connection. A change in feed can put more sulphur into a particular intermediate stream. Treating that stream to meet the final product specification may require more hydrogen and can increase the load on equipment that handles sulphur compounds. The available margin has to exist in the right units at the same time.

FOLLOW ONE STREAM TO ITS DESTINATION

Imagine an assay promising more middle distillate at a moment when diesel demand is strong. The purchasing case looks attractive until the planner learns that one treating unit will be under maintenance during the proposed run. The additional material may need storage, a different blending destination or a later processing window. Any of those can change the cargo’s value.

The useful conversation at the planning meeting is specific. Which unit would reach its constraint first? What product would accumulate? How much confidence is there in the assay for this cargo, particularly if the grade is blended from several sources? These are questions that operations, laboratory, scheduling and commercial teams can answer together.

WHEN THE CARGO REACHES THE UNIT

Tank inventory creates a delay between a scheduling change and the moment the new blend reaches a processing unit. A laboratory sample drawn from yesterday’s tank and a product yield recorded during today’s transition may describe different feedstocks. Before using the first operating results to revise the crude valuation, establish when the new blend actually reached the unit and how much previous material remained in the system. Otherwise a sound assay can look inaccurate for the wrong reason.

TAKEAWAY

A lower crude price is only the first line of the decision. Trace the expected products through the refinery and into saleable tanks. The limiting unit or unavailable tank often explains more about the buying decision than the discount quoted at the loading port.
