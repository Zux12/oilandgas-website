How One Pipeline Carries Several Fuels Without Mixing the Whole Batch

Oil & Gas Editorial

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-27

Summary

August 2026 edition · Gasoline and diesel can share a products pipeline. The commercial challenge sits in the short stretch where one batch meets the next and the receiving terminal must decide where it belongs.

Full article

A products terminal may be receiving diesel in the morning and expect gasoline next through exactly the same pipe. There need not be a solid divider travelling between them. The batches occupy consecutive lengths of pipeline, and most of each batch retains its identity. The boundary between them is where the work begins.

WHAT TRAVELS DOWN THE LINE

As the liquids move, a portion of the trailing product mixes with the leading one. Operators call this transition an interface. Its size and character depend on the products, the pipeline and the movement history. Good sequencing puts compatible products together where practical and considers what the receiving terminal can do with the material between them.

There is an economic reason to care about batch size. Suppose two schedules deliver the same total volume. One uses a few large batches and the other alternates grades more frequently. The second schedule creates more boundaries to manage. It may still be worthwhile if a customer urgently needs a particular grade or if terminal storage is tight. The cheapest pipeline schedule and the most useful delivery schedule can differ.

THE RECEIVER’S DECISION

The terminal follows the movement using the batch schedule, measured quantities and changes in product properties. Where adjacent products have distinguishable densities, a density trend can help locate the transition. Similar grades may require other evidence. An instrument indication has to be interpreted against product specifications and the operator’s procedures.

Interface material can sometimes be blended into a suitable product within quality limits. Other mixtures are segregated as transmix for processing or another approved disposition. Both choices require tank space or a planned destination. If an interface tank is unavailable when the batch arrives, a small portion of the movement can constrain a much larger delivery.

A RECEIPT THAT CHANGES THE SCHEDULE

Imagine that the next tank has enough space for the saleable diesel but the planned destination for its trailing interface is nearly full. Reporting only the free capacity in the diesel tank would make the movement look feasible. Reporting the capacity for each part of the batch exposes the constraint before dispatch commits the line. This is one reason pipeline nominations, product-quality information and terminal tank plans are more useful together than as three independent schedules.

TAKEAWAY

The success of a batch movement is measured at the receiving tank. Getting the right volume to the terminal is only part of the job. Dispatch and receiving teams also need a shared understanding of which grade will arrive, when its quality begins to change and where the transition material belongs.
