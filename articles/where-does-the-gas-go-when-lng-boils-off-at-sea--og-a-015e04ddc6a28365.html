Where Does the Gas Go When LNG Boils Off at Sea?

Oil & Gas Editorial

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-27

Summary

August 2026 edition · Even an insulated LNG cargo absorbs some heat. The resulting vapour links the cargo tanks, propulsion machinery and the quantity ultimately delivered.

Full article

A loaded LNG carrier leaves port with cold liquid in its cargo tanks and vapour above it. During the voyage, heat enters through the insulation. Some liquid evaporates. That change of state is expected, and the ship is designed to manage it. Whether the gas becomes engine fuel, returns to the cargo or follows another route depends on the vessel and what it is doing.

A CARGO AND A FUEL SYSTEM

On a suitably equipped ship, boil-off gas can supply propulsion or power generation. The ship then receives useful energy from part of the cargo. Other vessels can reliquefy some of the vapour and return it to the cargo tanks. Some designs also have a gas combustion unit to manage surplus vapour when the normal consumers cannot take it. These capabilities vary between ships.

Consider a vessel making good speed and then slowing while it waits for a berth. Propulsion demand may fall quickly. Heat continues to enter the tanks during the wait. A voyage plan therefore needs to consider periods when gas production and fuel demand move in different directions. The insulation specification alone cannot tell a charterer how much LNG will be delivered.

WHAT THE DELIVERY FIGURES MEAN

Boil-off describes a physical change. It does not automatically represent gas released to the atmosphere. Gas burned for propulsion, vapour reliquefied and gas remaining in the tanks all have different places in the cargo and energy accounts. The point at which the parties measure custody transfer also matters.

Take two similar voyages with different waiting times. Before attributing their delivery difference to insulation performance, examine the sailing profile, fuel records, tank inventories and any return of reliquefied gas. A change in one accounting category can explain an apparent loss in another.

TWO WAYS TO COUNT THE SAME VOYAGE

A voyage report might put propulsion fuel in tonnes while the cargo statement records delivered energy. Those numbers cannot be subtracted directly. The reviewer needs the agreed measurement basis, the quantity and composition information used for energy conversion, and the times to which the opening and closing inventories refer. A ship that reports its fuel use clearly gives the commercial team a better starting point for explaining the delivery, even before anyone compares the voyage against a performance guarantee.

TAKEAWAY

The useful question is where the vapour went over the whole voyage. Following it from the tank to its eventual use gives a clearer explanation of cargo performance than quoting a boil-off rate in isolation.
