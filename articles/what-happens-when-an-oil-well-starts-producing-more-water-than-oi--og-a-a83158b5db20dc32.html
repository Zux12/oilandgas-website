What Happens When an Oil Well Starts Producing More Water Than Oil?

Oil & Gas Editorial

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-27

Summary

July 2026 edition · Water cut can rise while total liquid production looks steady. A simple well example shows how the constraint can move from underground to the water-handling plant.

Full article

Imagine a well delivering 1,000 barrels of liquid a day. Yesterday’s production sheet showed the same number. At first glance, the well looks remarkably steady. The separator tells a different story if the proportion of oil in that liquid has fallen.

READING THE TWO NUMBERS

Water cut is the share of the produced liquid volume that is water, with volumes compared on a consistent basis. At 40 per cent water cut, our example well produces 400 barrels of water and 600 barrels of oil. At 70 per cent, it produces 700 barrels of water and 300 barrels of oil. The liquid total has stayed level while the saleable oil has halved. Actual production accounts also have to allow for measurement conditions and the quality of the separation.

Water may come from the producing formation or include water previously injected to support recovery. A rising water cut is an observation. Establishing its cause takes the well history, reservoir information and confidence in the measurements. A single sample after a change in flow may tell a different story from a properly conducted well test.

WHERE THE NEXT BARREL GOES

Once at surface, the additional water occupies separator capacity. It also needs an available treatment and management route. Depending on the field and its permissions, that can include reinjection, disposal or treatment for a permitted discharge. The route matters to the production decision. Spare capacity in the oil export line does little for a field whose water-treatment system is already full.

Consider the daily allocation meeting. One well might deliver more oil for the same volume of water handled than another. Before changing priorities, the team needs comparable well-test data, an understanding of reservoir consequences and a view of the entire facility. Yesterday’s water-cut figure alone cannot settle that choice.

WHAT THE BOTTLENECK COSTS

At 70 per cent water cut, every 100 barrels of total liquid in the example brings 30 barrels of oil and 70 of water to surface. If another well produces equal volumes of oil and water, the same 70 barrels of available water-handling capacity could accompany 70 barrels of its oil. That arithmetic helps explain why water capacity has an economic value. It leaves an important engineering question for the team: can each well actually deliver those rates over the period under review?

TAKEAWAY

Put oil rate, water rate and available water-handling capacity beside total liquid production. Together they show whether maintaining a headline production number is delivering oil or moving an increasing volume of water through the plant.
