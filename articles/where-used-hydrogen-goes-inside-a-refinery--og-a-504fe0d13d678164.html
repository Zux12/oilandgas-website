Where Used Hydrogen Goes Inside a Refinery

Oil & Gas Editorial

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-27

Summary

September 2026 edition · Some hydrogen reacts with the feed, some circulates through the unit again, and some leaves in a purge. Keeping those quantities separate gives a more useful picture of refinery hydrogen demand.

Full article

A refinery hydrogen compressor can handle far more gas than the site produces as fresh hydrogen. The two numbers describe different things. A large part of the hydrogen-rich stream in a hydroprocessing unit can circulate repeatedly. Only part is chemically consumed on any one pass.

FOLLOW THE MOLECULES

In hydrotreating, hydrogen participates in reactions such as sulphur removal. Some sulphur leaves the hydrocarbon molecules as hydrogen sulphide and is subsequently handled by the refinery’s treating systems. Other hydrogen becomes part of the hydrocarbon products. These chemically consumed quantities cannot simply be collected from a separator and returned to the reactor.

A portion of the hydrogen fed to the reactor remains unreacted. After the reactor effluent is cooled and separated, a hydrogen-rich gas stream can be treated as required and recycled. Fresh make-up hydrogen replaces consumption and other losses. The recycle stream also contains other gases, so its total flow and its hydrogen content answer different questions.

WHY SOME GAS LEAVES THE LOOP

If unwanted light components accumulate, the composition of the circulating gas changes. A controlled purge can help manage that accumulation. Depending on the refinery and the stream quality, purge gas may go to a recovery system, another suitable user or the fuel-gas network. Treating every unit of purge as pure, recoverable hydrogen would overstate the opportunity.

Suppose a monthly report shows a rise in recycle flow but little movement in fresh hydrogen purchases. Before calling that an efficiency loss, check the gas compositions, feed mix and measurement basis. A circulation increase can reflect a change in how the unit is operating. A rise in fresh consumption per comparable quantity of feed raises a different set of questions.

A BALANCE THE SHIFT CAN EXPLAIN

For a simplified steady unit, imagine 90 units of hydrogen returning in recycle and ten units arriving as fresh supply. The combined hydrogen feed is 100 units on the same basis. If 90 come back again, the remaining ten must be accounted for by consumption and net exits. They cannot all be claimed as a saving simply by reducing the recycle setting. A recovery proposal has to identify a real exit stream and demonstrate what useful fresh supply it could displace.

TAKEAWAY

Build the hydrogen balance around fresh supply, chemical consumption, exports and losses across a defined boundary. Show recycle separately. That distinction helps a refinery evaluate recovery projects and explain why a high compressor flow does not automatically imply an equally high fresh hydrogen requirement.
