The Gas Already in the Pipeline That Helps Meet the Evening Peak

Oil & Gas Editorial

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-27

Summary

August 2026 edition · Gas customers can draw faster than gas enters a transmission network for a time. Linepack explains where the difference comes from and why the network must later replenish it.

Full article

At the end of a gas day, the quantities entering and leaving a transmission network may be close. At six in the evening they can be far apart. Households, power generators and industrial users do not all consume gas at a steady rate. The gas already held under pressure inside the network helps bridge those differences.

INVENTORY INSIDE THE PIPE

Gas is compressible. A pipeline section with a fixed internal volume can contain different amounts of gas as its pressure, temperature and composition change. Operators use the term linepack for the inventory in the network. They estimate it using system information and express it on a common basis so that receipts, deliveries and stock can be compared.

For a simplified illustration, assume that 100 units enter during an hour and 110 units leave. With all quantities on the same basis and other flows set aside, linepack falls by ten units. Reverse the imbalance during a quieter hour and the inventory can recover. The example explains a balance sheet. It does not establish that any particular pipeline can serve that withdrawal.

WHY LOCATION MATTERS

A national inventory figure can hide a local delivery problem. Gas has to travel through a network with friction and changing pressure, and delivery points have operating requirements. Two networks with the same total linepack may have the gas distributed differently. What matters at a particular exit is whether the system can sustain delivery there while operating within its limits.

A useful morning review therefore asks two questions about yesterday’s evening peak. How much inventory did the network use? Where did the pressure response depart from the forecast? The first helps explain the daily balance. The second helps operators assess the next peak, especially if the replenishment expected overnight arrives later than planned.

THE NEXT MORNING MATTERS

If the evening’s ten-unit draw is followed by five hours in which receipts merely equal deliveries, the ten units have still to be restored. An operator looking only at the latest hour might see a balanced network, even though it has less inventory in hand for the following peak. A daily chart showing the opening stock, the lowest point and the closing stock tells that story much better than an average hourly flow.

TAKEAWAY

Linepack gives a transmission network flexibility across hours. It is a working inventory that has to be managed and restored. Reading it beside the forecast for receipts, demand and local delivery conditions makes the evening peak easier to understand.
