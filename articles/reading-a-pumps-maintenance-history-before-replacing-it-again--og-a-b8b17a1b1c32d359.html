Reading a Pump’s Maintenance History Before Replacing It Again

Oil & Gas Editorial

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-27

Summary

October 2026 edition · Three seal replacements can represent three unrelated failures or one recurring system problem. A timeline built from maintenance and operating records helps distinguish the two.

Full article

A centrifugal pump appears on the bad-actor list again. Three work orders mention seal leakage. The latest repair has restored service, and the annual maintenance cost points towards replacing the pump. Before preparing a purchase request, put the work orders on a timeline with the way the machine was actually used.

WHAT FAILED, AND WHEN?

A work order often records the component changed because that is what the store issued. It may say little about the physical evidence on the removed part, the operating state when leakage began or the maintenance done elsewhere at the same time. Three orders carrying the same code do not establish three identical failure mechanisms.

Add operating hours where available. A standby pump that runs for one week between repairs has a different history from a duty pump that runs continuously for months. Check whether the recorded dates mark the first symptom, the changeover or the workshop repair. Confusing these events can produce an impressive-looking interval that measures little about reliability.

PUT THE SYSTEM BACK IN THE PICTURE

Suppose two repairs followed a change in production rate, while the third followed a long idle period. That pattern suggests questions about the operating range, service conditions and standby management. It does not prove that any one of them caused the failures. The pump curve, system conditions and inspection evidence can help a competent team decide what to investigate.

The sister machine can be a useful comparison if the two units genuinely share duties and service conditions. A unit that usually runs at another flow or has a different seal arrangement is a weaker comparison, however similar its casing looks. Recording those differences may explain why an apparent fleet-wide problem is concentrated on one tag.

ONE NUMBER THAT CHANGES THE DISCUSSION

Two repairs in twelve calendar months sound like a six-month interval. If the machine accumulated only 400 running hours in that period, a running-hour view tells a different story. Neither measure alone explains the cause. Damage during idle periods would make calendar exposure important, while wear under load may track operating hours more closely. Putting both time bases on the same sheet lets the team test its theory against the duty the machine actually saw.

TAKEAWAY

Before replacing the pump, ask what the new machine would experience on its first day in the old system. If the proposed purchase leaves the suspected operating condition unchanged, the replacement case needs a better explanation of why reliability will improve.
