When Troubleshooting Should Stop

Oil & Gas Editorial

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-06

Summary

A fired-heater incident at Marathon Martinez highlights the importance of predetermined shutdown and withdrawal criteria when abnormal conditions become dangerous.

Full article

Based on a published US Chemical Safety Board (CSB) investigation. The discussion below is Oil & Gas Editorial commentary on the investigation, not a firsthand account.

WHAT HAPPENED

On 19 November 2023, a fired-heater tube ruptured during startup at the Marathon Martinez Renewables facility in California. The release of renewable diesel and hydrogen resulted in a fire that seriously injured an operator.

The CSB’s final report, released on 13 March 2025, addressed several safety issues. Its published findings described continued troubleshooting in dangerous conditions and an operator being directed to turn off burners manually near the heater. Recommendations included clearer responses to high tube metal temperatures, remote shutdown arrangements and criteria for withdrawing personnel.

A DECISION MADE BEFORE THE EVENT

Troubleshooting is an important operating skill. The difficulty is recognising when the effort to understand a problem is itself exposing people to increasing risk. That boundary is easier to discuss during a review than to establish during an urgent startup problem.

The editorial lesson is to make the decision process explicit before abnormal conditions occur. An alarm should connect to a defined response that operators can understand and carry out. The team also needs to know when further investigation must give way to the site’s shutdown and withdrawal procedures.

QUESTIONS FOR OPERATIONS AND ENGINEERING

A useful review begins with the response expected from each significant alarm. Does the procedure identify the action, the responsible role and the point at which escalation is required? Are personnel expected to approach equipment to perform that action, and has the associated exposure been assessed?

The conversation should include instrument engineers, process engineers and operators. Each group sees a different part of the problem. Instrument engineers understand how a condition is detected. Process engineers understand the developing hazard. Operators understand the practical demands of carrying out the response in the field.

Training scenarios can explore the point where information remains incomplete but a protective decision is still required. A scenario need not reward the participant who diagnoses the fault fastest. It can instead examine whether the team recognises its limits, communicates uncertainty and follows the established response.

KEEPING THE LESSON USEFUL

For a site discussion, choose one existing abnormal-operation procedure and trace its instructions from the control room to the field. Ask whether each action remains practical under the conditions the procedure is intended to address. Any proposed changes should then pass through the facility’s engineering and operational review processes.

TAKEAWAY

The ability to solve a problem is valuable. Equally valuable is an agreed point at which the team stops investigating and protects people. The Martinez case makes that decision boundary a concrete subject for startup and emergency-response reviews.
