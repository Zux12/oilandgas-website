When the Level Reading Tells the Wrong Story

Oil & Gas Editorial

Published on Oil & Gas: 2026-11-06

Summary

The Texas City refinery disaster shows why a plausible instrument reading must still be questioned when the process tells a different story. Lessons for startup readiness, alarm testing and operational decisions.

Full article

Based on a published US Chemical Safety Board (CSB) investigation. The discussion below is Oil & Gas Editorial commentary on the investigation, not a firsthand account.

WHAT HAPPENED

On 23 March 2005, an explosion occurred at BP’s Texas City refinery during startup of an isomerisation unit. The CSB investigation described a raffinate splitter tower that overfilled and released hydrocarbons through an atmospheric blowdown system. Fifteen people died.

The instrumentation did not give operators a dependable picture of the developing situation. One high-level alarm operated earlier in the startup, while another malfunctioned. The tower level transmitter later indicated a low and falling level even though the vessel was overfilled. The CSB identified wider organisational, design and operational deficiencies, so this was much more than an isolated instrument failure.

THE ENGINEERING LESSON

A number on a control-room display can look convincing because it is precise, stable and familiar. None of those characteristics proves that it represents the process correctly. Measurement assurance requires an understanding of what the instrument can actually measure and when its indication can become misleading.

For an instrumentation team, this case raises a useful distinction between checking a transmitter and checking a protection function. A calibration result addresses a particular measurement task. It does not, by itself, demonstrate that the complete alarm or protective function will detect the relevant hazardous condition and support the required response.

QUESTIONS FOR A STARTUP REVIEW

An editorial discussion of this case could begin with the information operators need before introducing inventory. Which instruments are essential to the startup decision? What evidence shows that those instruments and associated alarms are available? How are unresolved defects communicated between maintenance, operations and the person authorising startup?

The next discussion concerns conflicting evidence. A displayed level might disagree with inventory calculations, flow information or other process observations. Teams benefit from agreeing in advance how such conflicts will be escalated under their approved procedures. The decision should not depend on an individual having enough confidence to challenge a familiar screen during a busy startup.

Finally, previous abnormal startups deserve attention. A successful recovery can leave the impression that the operating method was acceptable. Reviewing the deviation, rather than only the eventual outcome, can expose weaknesses before the next startup.

TAKEAWAY

Reliable instrumentation includes the hardware, the testing arrangements and the decisions made when information is uncertain. This case is a useful prompt for a joint conversation between instrument engineers and operators about whether their startup picture is both complete and trustworthy.

These discussion points support learning and do not replace site-specific operating procedures or engineering assessment.
